# English: tells and plain style

Read this when editing English, or for the second pass on an English draft. It is a catalog for recognizing problems, not a checklist to run top to bottom.

Three rules of use, which outrank every specific rule below:

- **A sentence that already reads naturally stays.** Over-editing is a failure equal to under-editing. Three edits that matter beat thirty mechanical swaps.
- **Match the move, not the string.** Each family below is one move (for example, "invent a misreading, then correct it"). The listed phrases are its usual costumes. The same move in new words still counts; a listed word that carries information does not.
- **Density decides.** One device is a choice. Three in a paragraph, or one repeated through the piece, is a finding.

The author's own idiom, feeling, and conviction are content. Do not flatten them into neutral prose, and do not add slang or quirks to sound human.

## Tests

When unsure about a sentence, ask these instead of consulting a word list.

| Test | Question | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Portability | Could this sentence move to another document unchanged? | It carries no fact about this one. Replace it with a fact only this piece has, or cut it |
| Deletion | What does the reader lose if it goes? | Nothing: cut. A fact, a condition, or the author's stance: keep |
| Actor | Who does the verb? | Name them when the source says who. Keep the passive when the actor is unknown or beside the point |
| Mechanism | Does it say what the thing does, or how it feels? | Replace the feeling with the fact it displaced: "deploys feel effortless" becomes "a failed deploy rolls back within one minute" |
| First read | Would the reader have to parse it twice? | Split it or spell it out. A long sentence that parses on the first pass stays |
| Decision first | Does the first sentence of the piece, and of each section, carry the outcome? | Move the conclusion up; reasons and background follow |

## Families of tells

**1. Importance stapled on.** "Marks a turning point", "plays a vital role", "a testament to", "in today's fast-moving landscape"; promotional adjectives (seamless, robust, powerful, groundbreaking, vibrant); stakes inflation ("will fundamentally reshape"); a trailing -ing clause that explains significance ("..., highlighting the need for", "..., ensuring that"); "despite these challenges, X continues to thrive".
Fix: delete the claim and keep the fact. State the property, with a number where one exists.

**2. A setup in place of a statement.** Negative parallelism ("It's not X. It's Y.", "The question isn't A, it's B"); the self-answered question ("The result? Devastating."); false suspense ("Here's the thing", "Here's where it gets interesting"); faux insight ("what nobody tells you", "the real question is", "at its core"); the colon reveal ("The detail that makes it work: a separate grader"); an alternative nobody raised ("some might say", "a tempting approach would be").
Fix: say Y. Put the evidence next to it.
Keep: a contrast the reader really holds, or one the piece has walked through with material.

**3. Writing about the writing.** "In this section we will", "Let's dive in", "It's worth noting that", "As mentioned above", "In conclusion", "To sum up"; a first sentence that restates its heading; a paragraph that announces the list that follows. The hardest form to spot uses plain words and sits at paragraph boundaries: the draft rates its own evidence ("the benchmarks are less charming"), signposts ("the other half of the picture is less flattering"), labels a claim instead of making it ("one result complicates the picture"), or tells the reader what a quote proves before quoting it.
Test: is the grammatical subject the piece itself (the picture, the story, the evidence, the takeaway, this section) and not the thing written about? Then it is commentary.
Fix: delete it, or turn it into the thing it describes. "The open question is whether X holds" becomes "Forty hours of testing is not enough to tell whether X holds."
Keep: one orienting sentence in a long piece when the kind of content changes; a judgment about which of two findings should decide the matter, which is a claim about the world.

**4. Manufactured rhythm.** Three items every time; the same sentence opener three times running; rows of fragments ("No priors. No nostalgia. No rules."); several em dashes in a paragraph; a quotable one-liner closing every paragraph; a sentence that applauds the one before it ("And that matters.").
Fix: list as many items as are true. Write full sentences. One short sentence can land a point; a row of them is a pose.

**5. Vocabulary.** Model words (delve, leverage, utilize, harness, foster, underscore, showcase, navigate, tapestry, landscape, realm, journey, crucial, pivotal, moreover, additionally); long ways to say "is" or "has" (serves as, stands as, represents, boasts, offers); intensifiers that only signal emphasis (really, truly, genuinely, fundamentally, deeply, simply, actually); stacked hedges ("could potentially"); filler ("in order to", "due to the fact that", "at this point in time").
Fix: the plain word. One hedge, where the uncertainty is real. Keep an adverb that changes the claim ("explicitly set", "deliberately skipped").

**6. A figure where the mechanism belongs.** False agency ("the data wants a cleaner story", "the config knows about the queue"); borrowed metaphors for ordinary things (flywheel, north star, lever, surface, primitive, ecosystem); flourish where a literal phrase exists ("a dial worth turning" for "a parameter worth varying"); coined hyphenated adjectives and noun stacks the reader must decode ("a length-floor block drop"); "think of it as a Swiss Army knife"; invented labels that sound like established terms ("the supervision paradox"); "errors are handled gracefully".
Fix: name the operation and what the reader can check. Keep an analogy only if the paragraph collapses without it, it survives being pushed one level deeper, and the reader needs no further explanation.

**7. Borrowed authority and false scope.** "Experts believe", "studies show", "widely regarded" with no source; "from onboarding to billing" when those are two items and not the ends of a range; the same thing called three names in one passage.
Fix: name the source or cut the claim. List the items. Pick one term and repeat it; in technical writing repetition is precision.

**8. Formatting as decoration.** Every bullet opening with a bold label; bold sprinkled on nouns; Title Case Headings; emoji; bullets where two sentences would read better; a heading over a two-sentence section; horizontal rules between short sections.
Fix: bold only what a scanning reader must find. Sentence-case headings that state content. Lists for parallel items and steps; paragraphs for reasoning.

**9. Chat residue.** "Certainly!", "Great question", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if"; praise before the answer; "as of my last update"; an account of the steps the writer took that does not change what the reader does; bare counts and scorecards offered as proof of thoroughness.
Fix: delete. State what the source does not show, or say nothing.

Em dashes: default to none in text you generate. Use a comma, a period, a colon, or parentheses. Leave the author's own dashes alone.

## Sentences that move

Removing tells is half the job. These decide whether a paragraph reads easily.

- **Characters as subjects, actions as verbs.** "The team decided" rather than "a decision was reached by the team"; "we measured" rather than "measurement was performed".
- **Subject and verb early and close together.** Do not make the reader hold a long opening clause before learning who did what.
- **Old before new.** Begin a sentence with what the reader already has from the previous one; end it on the new, important information. Most "choppy" paragraphs fail this.
- **The paragraph's first sentence states its point.** The rest supports it.
- **Name the relation.** Where two clauses sit side by side, say how they relate: because, although, once, unless, so. Replace "also" and "moreover" with the actual relation, or with nothing.
- **Claim first, qualification after.** Caveats placed before the claim make the reader hold them with nothing to attach them to. "The treatment group improved significantly. The result is preliminary and rests on a small sample."
- **Concrete.** Names, numbers with units and a baseline, the actual command. "Finishes in 40 ms" rather than "runs quickly".
- **Calibrated.** If the evidence supports 70%, say 70% and admit the rest. Keep attributions, conditions, and limits; they carry the strength of the claim.
- **Write for a smart outsider.** The reader is as intelligent as you and does not happen to know this. Define the term at first use, give one example, and skip the hand-holding.

## If the draft was thought in Chinese

Habits that carry over from Chinese drafting and read as unidiomatic or evasive in English.

| Carry-over | Example | Write |
|---|---|---|
| Scene-setting opener | With the rapid development of AI, ... / Nowadays, ... / As we all know, ... | Start with the point |
| Topic before subject | For this problem, we have done much analysis | We analyzed the problem |
| Paired connectors | Although ..., but ... / Because ..., so ... | One connector |
| Verb turned noun | make an optimization on / carry out an investigation of | optimize / investigate |
| Formula phrases | actively promote, strive to, achieved remarkable results, play an important role, under the guidance of | What was done and what changed |
| Stock transitions | what's more, besides, last but not least, in a word, on the one hand ... on the other hand | Cut, or use "also", "but", "so" |
| "So-called" | the so-called agent framework | "So-called" signals doubt in English. Drop it unless doubt is meant |
| Vague growth | more and more users | The number, or "a growing share of" |
| Modesty formulas | just some immature thoughts; please kindly help to review | State the view; "Please review by Friday" |
| Translated parallelism | three balanced four-word phrases rendered one for one | Say it once, plainly |
| "such as ..., etc." | tools such as A, B, etc. | "such as A and B" |

Mechanical checks before returning English written from Chinese material:

- **Numbers.** 1 万 is 10 thousand; 1 亿 is 100 million; 12 亿 is 1.2 billion. Recompute each one; do not shift units by eye.
- **Dates and money.** 14 Nov 2026, never 11/14 or 14/11. Currency with its code: CNY 2,000.
- **Articles and plurals.** Check a/the and singular/plural on every noun phrase.
- **Punctuation.** No 、 or 《》 or full-width marks; no space before a comma or period; straight quotes.
- **Tense.** Past for what was done; present for what holds now.
- **Names.** People, products, and organizations in their official English form. Verify; do not transliterate from memory.
- **Placeholders.** A required item the source does not give is written in English: `[TO FILL: reply date]`.

## Reports and memos in English

Structure comes from `report.md`. These are the English conventions on top of it.

- The title or subject line is the conclusion or the ask: "Decision needed by 20 Nov: move cache to a cluster".
- Open with the bottom line: the recommendation, what you need from the reader, and by when. Then the single strongest reason. A summary runs five sentences at most.
- Make the ask explicit and answerable: "I need a yes or no on the extra CNY 2,000 a month by Friday 20 Nov."
- State status in plain words (on track, at risk, blocked) with the reason in the same sentence.
- Recommend in the active voice: "I recommend X because Y", not "it might be worth considering X".
- Bad news goes in the first paragraph, with what you are doing about it.
- Paragraphs of three to five sentences. Headings in sentence case that state the finding.
- Pick American or British spelling and hold it.

When a piece exists in both languages, write each version to read natively and compare them paragraph by paragraph for meaning, not length. The translation must not add an example, drop a hedge, turn "I did" into "you should", or append a moral the original did not have.
